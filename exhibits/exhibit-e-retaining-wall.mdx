Exhibit E documents the physical evidence, timeline of possession, and legal basis supporting the adverse possession and boundary-by-acquiescence claim of the 1501 Tulane Street property owners to the 4–5 foot strip along the northern boundary of their property. Following a hurricane in 2009, the concrete footings, structural posts, and wood overlays that had reinforced the original fence foundation were converted into a permanent retaining wall — a continuous, visible, structural boundary marker that has been in place for 17 years, well exceeding the 10-year statutory period under Texas Civil Practice and Remedies Code § 16.026.

<Warning>
**Viable Adverse Possession Claim — 17 Years of Continuous Possession:** The 2009 retaining wall — with its concrete footings, structural posts, and wood-overlay reinforcement — constitutes "actual and visible appropriation" of the 4–5 foot strip under Texas Civil Practice & Remedies Code § 16.021. The 17-year period of possession, combined with tacking across family ownership (2000–2009 and 2009–present), **far exceeds the 10-year statutory period** under § 16.026. The presence of poured concrete foundations and embedded posts defeats the "casual fence" defense. Any development that presupposes ownership of this strip without resolving the adverse possession claim proceeds on legally contested ground.
</Warning>

## Legal Basis — Detailed Citations

The family's boundary claims rest on four pillars of Texas property law. Each is reproduced in full below.

<Accordion title="Texas Civil Practice & Remedies Code § 16.021 — Definitions">

**Authority:** Texas Civil Practice & Remedies Code § 16.021

**Full citation:** Section 16.021 defines the key terms for adverse possession claims:

> "Adverse possession" means an actual and visible appropriation of real property, commenced and continued under a claim of right that is inconsistent with and is hostile to the claim of another person. [10]

> "Peaceable possession" means possession of real property that is continuous and is not interrupted by an adverse suit to recover the property. [10]

**Application to this case:** The 2009 retaining wall — with its concrete footings, structural posts, and wood overlays — constitutes an "actual and visible appropriation" of the 4–5 foot strip. It is a permanent, physical structure that gives visible notice to any neighboring owner that the land up to that line is claimed and possessed. The possession has been continuous and uninterrupted from 2009 to the present — a period of 17 years — and commenced under a claim of right that is inconsistent with and hostile to the claim of the current owner of 1505 Tulane Street.

</Accordion>

<Accordion title="Texas Civil Practice & Remedies Code § 16.026 — Adverse Possession: 10-Year Limitations Period">

**Authority:** Texas Civil Practice & Remedies Code § 16.026

**Full citation:** Section 16.026 provides:

> A person must bring suit not later than 10 years after the day the cause of action accrues to recover real property held in peaceable and adverse possession by another who cultivates, uses, or enjoys the property. [11]

> Peaceable possession of real property held under a duly registered deed or other memorandum of title that fixes the boundaries of the possessor's claim extends to the boundaries specified in the instrument. [11]

**Application to this case:** The family's possession of the retaining wall strip has been continuous and uninterrupted since 2009 — 17 years, well exceeding the 10-year statutory period. The period can be "tacked" across family ownership (sister's ownership 2000–2009; parents' ownership 2009–present), as Texas law permits tacking of successive possessory periods among predecessors in title. The combined family possession of 25+ years more than satisfies the § 16.026 threshold.

The owner of 1505 Tulane Street acquired the property in February/March 2026. Under § 16.026, the cause of action to recover the retaining wall strip accrued no later than 2009 — more than 10 years before the new owner's purchase. The 10-year period has expired. The new owner acquired 1505 Tulane subject to the family's vested adverse possession claim.

</Accordion>

<Accordion title="Schiele v. Kimball, 194 S.W. 944 (Tex. 1917) — Boundary Disputes as Trespass to Try Title">

**Authority:** Schiele v. Kimball, 194 S.W. 944 (Tex. 1917)

**Court:** Texas Supreme Court

**Full citation and holding:** The Texas Supreme Court in Schiele v. Kimball established the foundational principle that boundary disputes may be tried through the statutory action of trespass to try title [12]. The Court articulated the controlling test:

> The test for determining whether a case is one of boundary is: Would there have been any case if there had been no question of boundary? If not, it is necessarily a boundary case, though it may involve questions of title. [12]

The Court held that the location of the boundary line is the dominating question of the entire case, and that limitation-based title claims depend on where the boundary line is located [12].

**Application to this case:** The 1501/1505 Tulane boundary dispute is properly characterized as a **boundary case** under Texas law. The family's adverse possession claim up to the 2009 retaining wall is viable under the 10-year limitations period of § 16.026. The applicant's development cannot proceed on a boundary that is legally contested under this doctrine. Any forced construction that presupposes the applicant's claimed boundary line exposes the project to a trespass to try title action in which the dominating question — the location of the boundary — has not been resolved.

</Accordion>

<Accordion title="Gulf Oil Corp. v. Marathon Oil Co., 152 S.W.2d 711 (Tex. 1941) — Boundary by Acquiescence">

**Authority:** Gulf Oil Corp. v. Marathon Oil Co., 137 Tex. 59, 152 S.W.2d 711 (Tex. 1941)

**Court:** Texas Supreme Court

**Full citation:** Texas courts recognize the doctrine of boundary by acquiescence, derived from the Supreme Court's ruling in Gulf Oil Corp. v. Marathon Oil Co.:

> As we understand the doctrine of boundary by acquiescence, it must be shown that there was some uncertainty as to the true boundary, which resulted in a line being established (generally by a fence), and that thereafter the adjoining land owners acquiesced in and recognized this line as the true boundary line between them. [13]

**Elements of boundary by acquiescence:**

1. Uncertainty or dispute as to the true boundary location.
2. A line established (generally by a fence or physical structure) to resolve that uncertainty.
3. Mutual recognition and acquiescence by both adjoining landowners in that line as the true boundary over an extended period. [14]

**Application to this case:** The 2009 retaining wall — built on the former fence line, with concrete footings and structural reinforcement — satisfies these elements. The fence line existed for years before the hurricane, and the retaining wall has marked the boundary continuously since 2009. Both the family and the prior owner of 1505 Tulane St. acquiesced in this line for over a decade before the new owner's arrival. The new owner's unilateral rejection of this long-established line does not erase the years of mutual acquiescence that preceded his purchase.

**The Casual Fence Doctrine — Why It Does Not Apply Here:** Texas courts distinguish between a "designed enclosure" (which supports adverse possession) and a "casual fence" (which does not). However, the presence of **poured concrete foundations**, **embedded structural posts**, and **deliberate wood-overlay reinforcement** transforms the 2009 retaining wall from a casual fence into a permanent, designed enclosure. This defeats the casual fence defense and establishes the "actual, visible, and hostile" possession required under § 16.021.

</Accordion>

This result:

- Invalidates the setback, eave height, and massing compliance claims in both submittals (HP2026-0244 and HP2026-0248).
- Triggers IRC Table R302.1 fire-rating requirements for any exterior wall within 5 feet of the true property line.
- Affects the impervious cover calculation, which is tied to the lot area measured from the true property lines.
- Exposes any construction that physically removes or damages the 2009 retaining wall (with its concrete footings and posts) to a claim for structural damage to 1501 Tulane's drainage improvements.
