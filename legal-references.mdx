## Legal Citations by Category

<Accordion title="Texas Civil Practice & Remedies Code — Adverse Possession">

### Texas Civil Practice & Remedies Code § 16.021 — Adverse Possession: Definitions

Section 16.021 defines the key terms for adverse possession claims:

> **"Adverse possession"** means an actual and visible appropriation of real property, commenced and continued under a claim of right that is inconsistent with and is hostile to the claim of another person.

> **"Peaceable possession"** means possession of real property that is continuous and is not interrupted by an adverse suit to recover the property.

The 2009 retaining wall at 1501 Tulane Street — with its concrete footings, structural posts, and wood overlays — constitutes an "actual and visible appropriation" of the 4–5 foot strip. It is a permanent, physical structure that gives visible notice to any neighboring owner that the land up to that line is claimed and possessed.

---

### Texas Civil Practice & Remedies Code § 16.026 — Adverse Possession: 10-Year Limitations Period

Section 16.026 provides:

> A person must bring suit not later than 10 years after the day the cause of action accrues to recover real property held in peaceable and adverse possession by another who cultivates, uses, or enjoys the property.

> Peaceable possession of real property held under a duly registered deed or other memorandum of title that fixes the boundaries of the possessor's claim extends to the boundaries specified in the instrument.

The family's possession of the retaining wall strip has been continuous and uninterrupted since 2009 — 17 years, well exceeding the 10-year statutory period. The period can be "tacked" across family ownership (sister's ownership 2000–2009; parents' ownership 2009–present), as Texas law permits tacking of successive possessory periods among predecessors in title.

</Accordion>

<Accordion title="Texas Case Law — Boundary Disputes">

### Schiele v. Kimball, 194 S.W. 944 (Tex. 1917)

The Texas Supreme Court in *Schiele v. Kimball* established the foundational principle that boundary disputes may be tried through the statutory action of trespass to try title. The Court articulated the controlling test:

> The test for determining whether a case is one of boundary is: Would there have been any case if there had been no question of boundary? If not, it is necessarily a boundary case, though it may involve questions of title.

The Court held that the location of the boundary line is the dominating question of the entire case, and that limitation-based title claims depend on where the boundary line is located. This means:

- The 1501/1505 Tulane boundary dispute is properly characterized as a boundary case under Texas law.
- The family's adverse possession claim up to the 2009 retaining wall is viable under the 10-year limitations period of § 16.026.
- The applicant's development cannot proceed on a boundary that is legally contested under this doctrine.

---

### Gulf Oil Corp. v. Marathon Oil Co., 137 Tex. 59, 152 S.W.2d 711 (Tex. 1941) — Boundary by Acquiescence

Texas courts recognize the doctrine of boundary by acquiescence, derived from the Supreme Court's ruling in *Gulf Oil Corp. v. Marathon Oil Co.*:

> As we understand the doctrine of boundary by acquiescence, it must be shown that there was some uncertainty as to the true boundary, which resulted in a line being established (generally by a fence), and that thereafter the adjoining land owners acquiesced in and recognized this line as the true boundary line between them.

The elements of boundary by acquiescence are:

1. Uncertainty or dispute as to the true boundary location.
2. A line established (generally by a fence or physical structure) to resolve that uncertainty.
3. Mutual recognition and acquiescence by both adjoining landowners in that line as the true boundary over an extended period.

The 2009 retaining wall — built on the former fence line, with concrete footings and structural reinforcement — satisfies these elements. The fence line existed for years before the hurricane, and the retaining wall has marked the boundary continuously since 2009. Both the family and the prior owner of 1505 Tulane St. acquiesced in this line for over a decade before the new owner's arrival. The new owner's unilateral rejection of this long-established line does not erase the years of mutual acquiescence that preceded his purchase.

</Accordion>

<Accordion title="Texas Penal Code — Criminal Trespass">

### Texas Penal Code § 30.05 — Criminal Trespass

If any soil sampling was conducted on the disputed boundary strip or the neighboring 1501 Tulane St. property without the property owner's written permission, it constitutes criminal trespass under **Texas Penal Code § 30.05**. Texas courts have specifically held that conducting geophysical operations or soil sampling on someone's land is a "valuable property right which belongs exclusively to the owner."

A search of the City of Houston's Online Sold Permit database reveals that no building permits have been pulled for 1505 Tulane or 1505 1/2 Tulane — under any address variation or under the owner's name. If soil borings or geotechnical drilling has been performed on the property without the required Monitoring Well/Boring Permit (Chapter 40, Article XII), this constitutes unpermitted work. The Permitting Office should investigate whether geotechnical work has been performed on the site without proper authorization, and whether any such work encroached onto the neighboring 1501 Tulane St. property.

</Accordion>

<Accordion title="Title Insurance — Form T-1 and Survey Coverage">

### Form T-1 — Owner's Policy of Title Insurance (Texas)

Every Texas title insurance policy contains a standard exception in Item 2 of Schedule B:

> "Any discrepancies, conflicts, or shortages in area or boundary lines, or any encroachments or protrusions, or any overlapping of improvements."

This means the title policy **does not cover boundary disputes, encroachments, or adverse possession claims** — even if the buyer purchased the property in good faith. The title company specifically carved out this risk.

A buyer can pay an additional premium to have the standard boundary exception amended to read only "shortages in area" — this is called "survey coverage." However, even with survey coverage, the policy only insures against matters not shown on the survey and not known at the time of closing. If the buyer's survey showed the boundary as he claims it, and the title company amended the exception based on that survey, the policy would still not cover an adverse possession claim by an adjacent property owner because:

- The adverse possession is an unrecorded claim by a third party in possession.
- The buyer's own survey (the outlier survey discussed in the boundary analysis) may have misrepresented the boundary.
- Title policies typically exclude matters created by or known to the insured.

**The "inquiry notice" doctrine** cuts against the buyer. Texas courts hold that possession by a third party can trigger "inquiry notice" — meaning a buyer who sees someone else in visible possession of part of the property has a duty to investigate. The 2009 retaining wall, the concrete footings, the maintained rock strip, and 17 years of visible family maintenance were all observable on the property at the time of closing.

**Bottom line:** The title company's failure to flag the boundary dispute does not mean the dispute doesn't exist or that the buyer's position is secure. It means the dispute was unrecorded and therefore outside the scope of a standard title search — and the buyer's title policy specifically excludes this type of claim from coverage.

<Accordion title="Building Code and Fire Code Standards">

### IRC Table R302.1 — Fire-Rated Exterior Walls

Under **IRC Table R302.1**, any exterior wall located less than 5 feet from a property line must have a 1-hour fire-resistance rating (tested per ASTM E119/UL 263) from both sides. Walls located less than 3 feet from a property line generally permit 0% openings — no unprotected windows or doors.

The proposed site plan shows the addition's north (plat "East") side wall at **3′–6½″** from the north (plat "East") property line. The submitted elevation drawings show standard wall construction and window placements along the north (plat "East") elevation — not fire-rated assemblies. The Permitting Office should flag this during plan review.

This issue is compounded by the boundary dispute: if the true property line is even closer than the applicant claims, the fire separation distance shrinks further, increasing the fire-rating requirements.

<Accordion title="City of Houston Municipal Code">

### City of Houston Chapter 42 — Subdivisions & Development

Under **City of Houston Chapter 42** (Subdivisions & Development) and Chapter 33 (Historic Preservation), a developer cannot calculate FAR, total lot coverage, or cumulative setbacks across multiple lots unless those lots are legally tied or merged through a recorded Replat or a recorded Agreement to Bind Properties (unity-of-title covenant).

As of the date of this report, neither document is on file with the Harris County Clerk or the City of Houston Planning Commission. The City of Houston Permitting Center cannot legally release structural building permits for a single home spanning both lots until one of these processes is completed.

---

### City of Houston Chapter 33 — Historic Preservation

Under Chapter 33 (Historic Preservation), each legally separate lot must independently satisfy its own FAR, building coverage, open space, and setback requirements. The minimum single-side setback in the Houston Heights Historic District is **5 feet**. If the boundary dispute along the southern line reveals that the actual property line sits even 2 feet closer than the applicant's plan claims, the setback drops below the mandatory 5-foot minimum, triggering an immediate violation.

---

### City of Houston Chapter 40, Article XII — Monitoring Well/Boring Permit

If any boring was conducted in the public right-of-way (street, sidewalk, or easement), a **Monitoring Well/Boring Permit** from the City Engineer is required. This permit requires an application, a GIMS utility map, insurance certificates, a surety bond ($1,000 for the first facility, $750 each additional), and a fee.

The City of Houston's Geotechnical and Environmental Requirements place the burden on the Geotechnical Consultant to "ensure necessary permits are obtained before commencement of drilling" and to use the property owner's help to locate underground utilities before drilling begins. Additionally, at least 48 hours before any drilling, Texas811 must be called to mark underground utilities.

</Accordion>

<Accordion title="Houston Heights Historic District Design Guidelines">

### Houston Heights Historic District Design Guidelines, Section 5 (February 2018)

The Houston Heights Design Guidelines establish the following requirements directly implicated by the COA applications:

**Cumulative Side Setback (Two-Story Houses):**

> "Within the Houston Heights Historic Districts, the side setback is increased to a minimum of five feet on each side and a cumulative total of 10 feet for one-story houses and **15 feet for two-story houses**."

The submitted site plan drawings show approximately **12 feet cumulative** (3′–6½″ north (plat "East") + 8′–5 15/16″ south (plat "West")) — a face-of-drawing violation 3 feet below the minimum for two-story houses.

**Driveway Retention:**

The Design Guidelines specify that pavement and driveways are included in lot coverage calculations and that existing driveways on historic properties should be retained as part of the historic character of the district. The proposed plans do not retain the existing driveway at 1505 Tulane St.

**Rear Access and Parking Configuration:**

- Front parking pads are discouraged in the Houston Heights Historic District, as they disrupt the historic streetscape pattern and front yard character.
- An alley-loading garage generally must establish a minimum of **20 feet of clearance** from an opposing alley-loading garage door, the rear wall of a front-facing garage, or a fence; a 24-foot clearance is preferred.
- A front-facing garage located with its rear wall at the alley may have a zero-foot rear setback.

**Lot Coverage — Detached Garage:**

Detached garages up to 400 square feet are excluded from lot coverage calculations; detached garages over 400 square feet are included. Pavement and driveways are excluded from lot coverage.

**Compatible Massing and Height:**

> "The height of new construction must not be taller than the typical height of existing contributing structures in the context area."

> "The scale and proportions of the new construction… must be compatible with the typical scale and proportions of existing contributing structures in the context area."

> "A new construction shall not be constructed with more than one story in a historic district that is comprised entirely of one-story contributing structures."

When the context area is accurately described, the Tulane block consists of 1-story homes and camelbacks — not full 2-story buildings. The applicant's proposed full 2-story massing is not compatible with this context.

</Accordion>
