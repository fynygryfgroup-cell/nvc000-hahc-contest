## Section III.E — Why the Title Company Did Not Discover the Boundary Dispute at Closing

A natural question arises: if there is a boundary dispute or adverse possession claim, why didn't the title company discover it during the buyer's closing in February/March 2026? The answer lies in how Texas title insurance works — and it actually weakens the buyer's position rather than strengthening it.

<Accordion title="Point 1 — Title companies search recorded documents only">
Texas title insurance companies search the recorded public records in the Harris County Clerk's office — deeds, liens, judgments, easements, mortgages, and other recorded instruments. An adverse possession claim that has never been recorded (no lawsuit filed, no affidavit recorded, no lis pendens notice) does not appear in a title search. The 2009 retaining wall and the family's 17 years of maintenance are physical facts on the ground, but they were never recorded in the deed records — so the title company would never have found them [5].
</Accordion>

<Accordion title="Point 2 — The standard Texas title policy excludes boundary disputes">
Every Texas title insurance policy contains a standard exception in Item 2 of Schedule B:

> "Any discrepancies, conflicts, or shortages in area or boundary lines, or any encroachments or protrusions, or any overlapping of improvements." [6][7]

This means the title policy does not cover boundary disputes, encroachments, or adverse possession claims — even if the buyer purchased the property in good faith. The title company specifically carved out this risk.
</Accordion>

<Accordion title="Point 3 — Survey coverage does not cure the problem">
A buyer can pay an additional premium to have the standard boundary exception amended to read only "shortages in area" — this is called "survey coverage." However, even with survey coverage, the policy only insures against matters not shown on the survey and not known at the time of closing [8]. If the buyer's survey showed the boundary as he claims it, and the title company amended the exception based on that survey, the policy would still not cover an adverse possession claim by an adjacent property owner because:

- The adverse possession is an unrecorded claim by a third party in possession.
- The buyer's own survey (the outlier survey discussed in Section III.D) may have misrepresented the boundary.
- Title policies typically exclude matters created by or known to the insured.
</Accordion>

<Accordion title="Point 4 — The 'inquiry notice' doctrine cuts against the buyer">
Texas courts hold that possession by a third party can trigger "inquiry notice" — meaning a buyer who sees someone else in visible possession of part of the property has a duty to investigate [9]. The 2009 retaining wall, the concrete footings, the maintained rock strip, and 17 years of visible family maintenance were all observable on the property at the time of closing. If the buyer or his surveyor observed these physical features and failed to investigate why the adjacent property owner's structures and improvements extended to what the buyer claims is his property line, the buyer may be charged with inquiry notice of the adverse possession claim.
</Accordion>

<Accordion title="Point 5 — What this means for the buyer's title position">
The buyer's title insurance does not protect him against the family's adverse possession or boundary-by-acquiescence claim because:

- The standard boundary exception in his policy excludes exactly this type of dispute.
- Even with survey coverage, unrecorded claims by parties in possession are not covered.
- The buyer's own survey (which had to be redone after he was dissatisfied with the initial results) may have misrepresented the boundary, and the title company relied on that survey in issuing the policy.
- The physical evidence of the family's possession was visible at closing, potentially triggering inquiry notice.
</Accordion>

<Warning>
**Bottom Line — Title Insurance Provides No Shield**

The title company's failure to flag the boundary dispute does **not** mean the dispute doesn't exist or that the buyer's position is secure. It means the dispute was unrecorded and therefore outside the scope of a standard title search — and the buyer's title policy **specifically excludes** this type of claim from coverage.

The confluence of four factors leaves the applicant without title insurance protection on the boundary dispute: (1) the standard Schedule B boundary exception carves out exactly this type of claim; (2) survey coverage cannot cure an unrecorded adverse possession claim by a party in visible possession; (3) the outlier survey that formed the basis for any survey coverage amendment was produced under disputed circumstances; and (4) the visible physical evidence of the family's 17-year possession triggered inquiry notice at closing that the buyer was obligated to investigate.
</Warning>
