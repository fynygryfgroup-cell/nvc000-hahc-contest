The 1501 Tulane Street family's boundary claims are not merely a disagreement about survey lines — they are grounded in over 17 years of continuous, visible, structural possession of the disputed strip, supported by Texas statutory adverse possession law, the doctrine of boundary by acquiescence, and the physical record of permanent improvements to the land. The 2009 retaining wall, with its concrete footings and embedded structural posts, stands as durable evidence of a boundary that two successive sets of neighbors recognized for over a decade before the current applicant arrived.

## Section IV.A — The 4–5 Foot Retaining Wall Strip

Following a hurricane in 2009, a fence line along the northern boundary of 1501 Tulane St. fell. The concrete footings, structural posts, and wood overlays that had reinforced the original fence foundation were converted into a permanent retaining wall. This structure has served as a continuous, visible, structural boundary marker since 2009 — 17 years of uninterrupted possession.

Under Texas Civil Practice & Remedies Code § 16.026 (the 10-Year Statute of Limitations for Adverse Possession), this meets the requirements for actual, visible, continuous, and hostile possession. The period of possession can be "tacked" across family ownership (sister's ownership 2000–2009; parents' ownership 2009–present), easily exceeding the 10-year statutory requirement.

The presence of poured concrete foundations, embedded posts, and deliberate structural reinforcement defeats the "casual fence" defense that landowners typically raise in adverse possession cases. These permanent improvements demonstrate substantial intent to enclose and permanently claim that line.

Additionally, under the doctrine of Boundary by Acquiescence, if adjacent landowners historically treated a fixed physical structure as the true division line over a long period, Texas courts will recognize that structure as the permanent boundary.

## Section IV.B — The Additional 5-Foot Strip (North of the Retaining Wall)

The family has also maintained an additional 5-foot strip north of the retaining wall since 2009, including laying rock underlayment to prevent flooding of an illegal storage drain and ongoing weed abatement. Under Texas law, routine landscaping and maintenance on unfenced land is generally treated as permissive or neighborly upkeep rather than exclusive, hostile possession. The retaining wall itself defines the boundary of structural possession; maintenance beyond the wall carries a weaker legal claim.

## Section IV.D — Legal Framework: Texas Statutory and Common-Law Authority

The family's boundary claims rest on three pillars of Texas property law:

<Accordion title="Pillar 1 — Texas Civil Practice & Remedies Code § 16.021: Definitions">
Section 16.021 defines the key terms for adverse possession claims:

> "Adverse possession" means an actual and visible appropriation of real property, commenced and continued under a claim of right that is inconsistent with and is hostile to the claim of another person. [10]

> "Peaceable possession" means possession of real property that is continuous and is not interrupted by an adverse suit to recover the property. [10]

The 2009 retaining wall — with its concrete footings, structural posts, and wood overlays — constitutes an "actual and visible appropriation" of the 4–5 foot strip. It is a permanent, physical structure that gives visible notice to any neighboring owner that the land up to that line is claimed and possessed.
</Accordion>

<Accordion title="Pillar 2 — Texas Civil Practice & Remedies Code § 16.026: Adverse Possession (10-Year Limitations Period)">
Section 16.026 provides:

> A person must bring suit not later than 10 years after the day the cause of action accrues to recover real property held in peaceable and adverse possession by another who cultivates, uses, or enjoys the property. [11]

> Peaceable possession of real property held under a duly registered deed or other memorandum of title that fixes the boundaries of the possessor's claim extends to the boundaries specified in the instrument. [11]

The family's possession of the retaining wall strip has been continuous and uninterrupted since 2009 — 17 years, well exceeding the 10-year statutory period. The period can be "tacked" across family ownership (sister's ownership 2000–2009; parents' ownership 2009–present), as Texas law permits tacking of successive possessory periods among predecessors in title.
</Accordion>

<Accordion title="Pillar 3 — Schiele v. Kimball, 194 S.W. 944 (Tex. 1917): Boundary Disputes as Trespass to Try Title">
The Texas Supreme Court in *Schiele v. Kimball* established the foundational principle that boundary disputes may be tried through the statutory action of trespass to try title [12]. The Court articulated the controlling test:

> The test for determining whether a case is one of boundary is: Would there have been any case if there had been no question of boundary? If not, it is necessarily a boundary case, though it may involve questions of title. [12]

The Court held that the location of the boundary line is the dominating question of the entire case, and that limitation-based title claims depend on where the boundary line is located [12]. This means:

- The 1501/1505 Tulane boundary dispute is properly characterized as a boundary case under Texas law.
- The family's adverse possession claim up to the 2009 retaining wall is viable under the 10-year limitations period of § 16.026.
- The applicant's development cannot proceed on a boundary that is legally contested under this doctrine.
</Accordion>

<Accordion title="Pillar 4 — Boundary by Acquiescence: Gulf Oil Corp. v. Marathon Oil Co., 152 S.W.2d 711 (Tex. 1941)">
Texas courts recognize the doctrine of boundary by acquiescence, derived from the Supreme Court's ruling in *Gulf Oil Corp. v. Marathon Oil Co.*, 137 Tex. 59, 152 S.W.2d 711 (1941):

> As we understand the doctrine of boundary by acquiescence, it must be shown that there was some uncertainty as to the true boundary, which resulted in a line being established (generally by a fence), and that thereafter the adjoining land owners acquiesced in and recognized this line as the true boundary line between them. [13]

The elements of boundary by acquiescence are:

1. Uncertainty or dispute as to the true boundary location.
2. A line established (generally by a fence or physical structure) to resolve that uncertainty.
3. Mutual recognition and acquiescence by both adjoining landowners in that line as the true boundary over an extended period. [14]

The 2009 retaining wall — built on the former fence line, with concrete footings and structural reinforcement — satisfies these elements. The fence line existed for years before the hurricane, and the retaining wall has marked the boundary continuously since 2009. Both the family and the prior owner of 1505 Tulane St. acquiesced in this line for over a decade before the new owner's arrival. The new owner's unilateral rejection of this long-established line does not erase the years of mutual acquiescence that preceded his purchase.

**The Casual Fence Doctrine and Why It Does Not Defeat This Claim**

Texas courts distinguish between a "designed enclosure" (which supports adverse possession) and a "casual fence" (which does not). However, the presence of poured concrete foundations, embedded structural posts, and deliberate wood-overlay reinforcement transforms the 2009 retaining wall from a casual fence into a permanent, designed enclosure. This defeats the casual fence defense and establishes the "actual, visible, and hostile" possession required under § 16.021.
</Accordion>
